# algomonster: Pricing, Free Content, and How to Tell If the Pattern-Based Prep Fits Your Timeline

Most people who type "algomonster" into a search bar aren't browsing for fun. They're usually somewhere between 40 and 400 LeetCode problems with an interview on the calendar, and they've hit one of two walls. Either the problem list feels infinite, or they can solve problems they've seen before and freeze on ones they haven't.

AlgoMonster is the site people land on at that moment. It sells a specific promise: instead of grinding volume, you learn the handful of patterns that interview problems are built from, plus flowcharts for recognizing which pattern a new problem needs.

Whether that's worth $99, $189, or nothing at all depends on what's actually inside, what's clearly not, and how much time you have. Here's the breakdown.

## What AlgoMonster is, in one paragraph

It's a text-and-diagram course, not a problem bank. The content is written by engineers who came out of Google and competitive programming, and the current course catalog is advertised on the subscribe page as **500+ lessons across 6 courses, 1,000+ illustrations, and 3,000+ editorials**. There is no video. You read, you look at diagrams, and you type code into an in-browser editor.

That format decision explains most of the love-it-or-leave-it split in third-party writeups. People who read fast and want to reference material later tend to like it. People who learn from watching someone solve a problem out loud usually don't.

## What you're actually buying

Pattern curriculum. The core product is organized around problem patterns rather than topics in the abstract — sliding window, two pointers, binary search variants, tree and graph traversal, dynamic programming, and so on. Each pattern comes with the reasoning behind it, a reusable code template, and practice problems that apply the template with small twists.

Decision flowcharts. These get less attention than they deserve. Instead of reading a solution and nodding, you walk through questions like "is the input sorted?" or "am I looking for a contiguous subarray?" until the flowchart points at a likely pattern. That's the actual interview skill — picking an approach in the first five minutes rather than the last fifteen.

AI assistant, unlimited on paid plans. It's embedded in the lessons rather than sitting in a corner as a chatbot. Select a chunk of content and get a plain-language explanation; submit code and it points at the error. The honest framing: this is a hint engine, not a substitute for understanding, and unlimited usage is one of the more concrete differences between paying and not.

Speedrun. Timed review sessions that cycle through problems with a clock running. It's a reasonable way to rehearse under mild pressure, though the problem pool is smaller than a contest platform's, so treat it as a warm-up rather than a simulation of a real interview loop.

Bonus courses. Paid plans now include a Foundation course covering data structures and algorithms from the ground up, and a Build an LLM course that walks through tokenization, attention, and training rather than stopping at API calls. Neither has anything to do with your coding screen, but they're part of the bundle.

Real questions from major tech companies, refreshed monthly. Useful for a sanity check on what's actually showing up, less useful than a dataset with frequency counts.

## What's not included — and this part matters

Every published review I found on the platform lands on the same three gaps, and the site doesn't dispute them.

**No video content at all.** This is deliberate. If you learn by watching someone reason through a problem on screen, the text-and-diagram format will feel like reading a technical manual in a library with no windows.

**Smaller problem set than a problem bank.** AlgoMonster's volume is measured in hundreds, not LeetCode's thousands. You can't use it to build the raw fluency that comes from solving 500 problems, because there aren't 500 to solve.

**System design and behavioral coverage is introductory.** The site's own marketing focuses on algorithms. Engineers targeting senior loops, where a dedicated system design round is a standard part of the process, should budget separate time and possibly a separate resource for that round. The same goes for behavioral prep — AlgoMonster was not built around STAR-format storytelling.

There's also no live coaching, no mock interviews with real engineers, and no way to ask a human a question when you're stuck on a concept. It's self-paced and self-guided, which is either the whole point or a dealbreaker depending on how you learn.

If any of those three gaps are your main problem, stop here and look elsewhere. If pattern selection speed is your main problem, keep reading.

## What you can get for free

You don't have to pay to find out whether the writing style works for you, and this is the least-publicized part of the platform.

The free tier gives you a limited slice of the lessons and problems, with no card required — enough to sit through a couple of patterns and decide whether text-plus-diagram teaching clicks. There's also a set of publicly accessible problem write-ups, published at `algo.monster/liteproblems/{leetcode problem number}`, which show up high in search results for specific questions. Those are individual solutions, not the structured curriculum, so they're more useful for checking how the site explains things than for learning patterns end to end.

## AlgoMonster vs. LeetCode, without the tribal nonsense

They're not competing for the same job. LeetCode is a gym. AlgoMonster is a textbook with exercises.

|  | AlgoMonster | LeetCode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary function | Teaches pattern selection through structured lessons | Provides volume and judge-based practice |
| Problem count | Hundreds, each tied to a pattern | Thousands, unsorted |
| Video | None — text and diagrams | User-made, inconsistent quality |
| System design | Introductory | Separate paid track, not its core strength |
| Price | $99/year or $189 lifetime | Free tier; Premium around $159/year |
| Defined finish line | Yes — roughly 4 to 8 weeks at 1–2 hours a day | None |

The combination that shows up over and over in 2026 reviews is AlgoMonster first for the mental model, LeetCode after for volume. That's not fence-sitting; it reflects the actual failure modes. Candidates who only grind volume often can't classify an unfamiliar problem. Candidates who only read a course often can't write the code fast enough under a clock.

If your budget is one subscription, the decision comes down to which of those two failures you're currently having.

## The money question: yearly or lifetime?

The yearly plan is $99. The lifetime plan is $189, charged once. Lifetime costs $90 more than a single year, which means it becomes the cheaper option somewhere in the second year.

Whether you get there depends on your interview pattern, and most engineers know their own pattern better than any review can guess it. If you're prepping for one specific loop over the next two months, or you just want a framework to review before occasional interviews, the yearly plan is the lower-commitment choice and you can let it lapse. If you interview every couple of years, change jobs, or simply don't want to think about a renewal appearing on your card, $90 extra for permanent access is a straightforward trade.

One thing the lifetime plan does protect you from: future price increases on your own access, since you've already paid.

## Who this is genuinely right for

Reading through the feature list and the published reviews, the profile that gets value out of AlgoMonster looks fairly specific.

You can already write working code in at least one language. This is not a programming fundamentals course, even with the Foundation course bundled in. If you need to learn what a hash map is, you're paying for the wrong thing.

Your problem is selecting an approach, not implementing it. The most common way prepared candidates fail a screen is burning fifteen of forty-five minutes on approaches that don't fit the problem's structure. A pattern catalog with decision flowcharts is aimed exactly at that failure.

You have four to eight weeks and can give it one to two hours a day. The curriculum is sized for that window. Two-week sprints tend to leave the decision logic shallow; three-month timelines tend to lose retention before the interview arrives.

You'd rather read than watch. This one is non-negotiable, because there's no video to fall back on.

If you're targeting senior roles where system design carries equal weight, you still need something else for that round — and that's a budget decision, not a knock on this product.

## The short version

AlgoMonster is a well-organized patterns course with a narrow, clearly-stated purpose. It will not make you fast on its own, it has no video, and its system design content is not built for senior loops. What it does have is structure, ~500 lessons across six courses, flowcharts that address the actual bottleneck in coding screens, and a price point that sits below LeetCode Premium at current promotional rates.

Spend an hour in the free tier. If the format clicks, the yearly plan covers a focused job search and the lifetime plan covers the next decade of them. If you find yourself wishing someone would just explain it in a video, you've learned something useful for the price of your own time.
